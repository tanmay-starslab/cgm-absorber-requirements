How a spectrum is made

Light from a distant quasar crosses the gas around a galaxy. Follow one beam, cell by cell, from the simulation to the spectrum a telescope would record.

01Chapter 1: Why can't we just look at the gas?

Galaxies sit inside gas too faint to see

A quasar far behind shines through it. We follow one beam through one of 198 simulated galaxies.

Figure: The starlight of one simulated galaxy, seen close up. The view pulls back until the whole halo of faint gas around it fills the frame, out to a dashed circle 246 kiloparsecs across from the centre. A quasar appears far behind.

Galaxy 488530 from TNG50-1 at z = 0, somewhat bigger than the Milky Way; its halo reaches R200c = 246 kpc.

A light behind reveals it

Gas between us and the quasar takes a little of its light away. Nothing is taken yet: all the light gets through.

Figure: A warm white beam leaves the quasar and crosses the halo toward us. A thin strip appears along the bottom: the beam's spectrum, a flat line at 100 percent, because all the light gets through.

The gas also glows (Lyα nebulae, O VI), but too faintly to map routinely; against a bright quasar it shows up in absorption.

02Chapter 2: What does one beam see?

One beam, 202 000 light-years from the centre

Seen from the side, the sightline passes 61.9 kpc from the galaxy's centre and crosses the whole halo.

Figure: The halo seen from the side. The beam runs straight across it from the quasar on the right toward our telescope on the left. A dashed marker from the centre to the beam reads 202 000 light-years.

b = 61.9 kpc, azimuth 62.1°, path from −R200c to +R200c = 493 kpc. Only gas within R200c is counted; real sightlines also cross gas beyond it, and our own Galaxy.

Every beam crosses different gas

Other catalogued sightlines of this galaxy flash by. Each meets different gas, so their spectra differ a lot. We follow one.

Figure: Faint lines flash one after another across the halo: other sightlines through the same galaxy. Our beam stays lit.

The catalogue samples many impact distances and orientations; the explorer's gallery shows every one.

03Chapter 3: What is the model galaxy made of?

The beam crosses 672 gas cells

The simulation stores gas in cells. Along its path the beam passes through 672 of them, lit as the light arrives.

Figure: A horizontal beam with a ruler of 493 kiloparsecs below it. Soft beads appear along it from right to left, one per gas cell, sized by the path inside the cell and coloured by temperature; a counter reaches 672.

Cells are soft, overlapping kernels, larger than real clouds; the path inside a cell has median 0.16 kpc and maximum 6.5 kpc.

Each has its own density, heat, speed

Scroll to move the probe along the beam. Every cell carries a density, a temperature, a metal content and a velocity.

Figure: A probe ring glides along the beam and a small card reads the cell under it: density, heat in kelvin, metals compared with the Sun, and speed in kilometres per second.

n_H = X_H ρ / m_H, with the hydrogen mass fraction X_H of each cell between 0.724 and 0.760.

Colour cells by

A few cells make most of the absorption

Half of the O VI absorption comes from 4 cells, half of the neutral hydrogen from 3.

Figure: Most beads dim. A few light up with orange rings for O VI and indigo rings for neutral hydrogen: 4 and 3 cells hold half of each.

Cells ranked by their own N = n dl: the fewest cells whose N adds up to half of the ion's total.

04Chapter 4: Which atoms take the light?

Cool clumps keep neutral hydrogen

This cell is 13 160 K. A share of 0.29 of its hydrogen is still neutral, and only neutral hydrogen takes this light.

Figure: One cell from the beam opens into a disc of hydrogen atoms. About 0.29 of them light up indigo: atoms that still hold their electron.

n(H I) = x_HI n_H, with x_HI from the simulation's own recipe.

Warm-hot gas: oxygen stripped of five electrons

These colours are ultraviolet light only space telescopes see. At 2.87×10⁵ K, 0.23 of this cell's oxygen is O VI.

Figure: The warm-hot cell holding the most O VI opens up and some of its oxygen atoms light orange. Beside it, a chart of the share of oxygen in O VI against temperature for five densities, with this cell marked; above about 500 000 kelvin the share falls.

n(O VI) = (O/H)☉ (Z/Z☉) n_H f_OVI(n_H, T), (O/H)☉ = 4.90×10⁻⁴, Z☉ = 0.01295, HM2012 table. Hydrogen's neutral share comes from the simulation, oxygen's from a table with a slightly different UV glow.

Each ion takes only photons fitting its step

An electron can only jump between fixed levels, so each ion takes one exact colour: H I at 1216 Å, O VI at 1032 Å.

Figure: Two energy ladders, H I and O VI. Three light waves arrive at each; the one whose wavelength fits the step is taken and the electron jumps up, the others pass by.

λ = hc/ΔE; f is how strong the jump is, Γ how long the upper level lives.

05Chapter 5: How does one cloud make a dip?

Each gas cell leaves a shadow

The beam spreads into ultraviolet colours our eyes cannot see. Where oxygen ions sit, one colour goes dark.

Figure: The beam fans out into a false-colour band labelled ultraviolet, colours our eyes cannot see. A dark stripe appears in the band. The band then folds down into a plot of light getting through, and the stripe becomes a dip.

Each cell removes light as e^(−τ); the dip's shape is a Gaussian in speed for O VI.

Moving gas shifts its shadow, like a siren

Gas moving away makes the dip slide to longer wavelengths. So the axis can read as speed toward or away.

Figure: The bottom axis starts in wavelength, 1031 to 1033 angstrom. As one cell's dip slides sideways, the axis relabels itself as speed toward or away, in kilometres per second.

V in km/s in the galaxy's frame; v_sys = −69.31 km/s puts the galaxy at V = 0.

Amount sets how much light is taken

More oxygen ions along the beam take a bigger bite: the shaded area of the dip grows.

Figure: One dip from cell 263 with its area shaded. A vertical arrow labelled amount grows, and the shaded area grows with it. At the centre about 18 percent of the light gets through.

Cell 263: log N = 14.16, b = 17.3 km/s, f = 0.133, λ = 1031.912 Å, so τ₀ = 1.73 and 18 % of the light gets through at the centre.

Heat spreads it out

Hotter ions jiggle faster, so the shadow spreads over more speeds. Hydrogen at the same heat is four times wider.

Figure: The same dip with a horizontal span labelled width. The width grows while the shaded area stays the same, so the dip gets shallower. A faint second dip shows hydrogen at the same heat, about four times wider.

This model uses heat only (b_turb = 0), so each cell's dip is as narrow as it can be. Cell 263: T = 2.87×10⁵ K, b = 17.3 km/s; hydrogen at that T has b = 69 km/s.

06Chapter 6: Why is the spectrum a speed map?

Where a dip sits tells its speed

Threads join each cell on the beam to its dip. Position in the spectrum is speed, not distance.

Figure: Top: the beam drawn as a ruler in kiloparsecs with its gas cells. Bottom: the spectrum against speed. Thin threads join each cell to the dip it makes.

Speeds are the gas's own motions; the slow expansion of space across the halo (up to 17 km/s here) is not added.

Far apart, same speed, same dip

Two cells 84 kpc apart on the beam move at about the same speed, so their shadows land together.

Figure: Two threads, from cells 84 kiloparsecs apart on the ruler, cross and meet in the same dip of the spectrum.

Cells 296 (l = −18.2 kpc) and 438 (l = 65.7 kpc) differ in speed by less than either dip's width.

07Chapter 7: How do many cells make a few dips?

Dozens of shadows merge into a few dips

The beam crosses 672 cells; 49 leave a visible shadow. Their shadows add, cell after cell, along the path.

Figure: Faint shadows from single cells appear one by one in path order and stack into a total. The light getting through then forms a few broad dips.

Peak τ = 3.06: O VI here is mostly unsaturated, and its deepest core is just starting to saturate.

Motion, not heat, makes this width

Single dips have b = 5.5 to 27.6 km/s, yet the trough spans −199 to +190 km/s. Cells moving differently spread it.

Figure: The whole O VI trough, far wider than any single cell's dip, with a few narrow single-cell dips drawn faintly inside it for comparison.

Total log N = log₁₀ Σ N = 14.77; rest equivalent width 435 mÅ.

08Chapter 8: What happens when a dip turns black?

More gas, deeper dip

Add more absorbing atoms to one dip and it deepens, while its width hardly changes.

Figure: One test dip with two meters beside it, depth and width. The amount rises from ten to the 12 toward ten to the 13.5 per square centimetre; the depth meter climbs to about 70 percent while the width barely changes.

τ₀ grows in proportion to N; the centre lets through e^(−τ₀).

Once black, only wider

On this beam, hydrogen's dip is black: adding more only widens it, so the amount is hard to read.

Figure: The test dip is black at its centre. As the amount rises from ten to the 13.5 to ten to the 20, the depth meter reaches 100 percent and stays pinned while the width meter grows; faint wings appear near the end. Then the real hydrogen trough on this beam replaces it, black across its core.

H I total log N = 18.04 (above 17.2: a Lyman-limit system), peak τ = 22 723. On this sightline every fit lands +0.10 to +0.34 dex off; in general a black dip gives only a rough amount, often just a lower limit.

09Chapter 9: What does the telescope do to it?

A blurry telescope merges dips

Every telescope smears the spectrum a little. Less sharp means neighbouring dips blend into one.

Figure: The O VI spectrum with small ticks marking the cells that matter. A grey bell, the telescope's blur, widens as the sharpness R falls from 150 000 to 5 000, and separate dips merge.

FWHM = 2.0, 10.0, 20.0, 60.0 km/s at R = 150 000, 30 000, 15 000, 5 000. A real instrument's blur (e.g. HST/COS) has broad wings a Gaussian misses.

Blur turns black dips grey

Blurred, the darkest dip looks grey, as if light were still getting through. The blur hides how dark it is.

Figure: The darkest dip on this beam, almost black at high sharpness, turns grey at its floor as the blur widens; a label reads how much light it now seems to let through.

absreq compares this with Doppler profiles of b_fid = √(b_th² + (5 km/s)²); the explorer uses Voigt profiles with thermal b only.

10Chapter 10: Why is it noisy?

Longer exposures beat the noise

Twice as clean needs four times as long. The noise pattern here stays the same; only its size shrinks.

Figure: The spectrum broken into pixels, five per resolution element. One fixed noise pattern shrinks as the signal to noise ratio climbs from 2 to 100.

S/N per resolution element; σ_pix = 0.22 at S/N 10, pixel 2.0 km/s at R 30 000. Noise is the same everywhere and flux is not allowed below zero; real detectors differ.

Noise can hide dips, and invent them

At low signal a weak dip vanishes, and a chance wiggle can look like one. Toggle the truth to compare.

Figure: A weak dip is highlighted. At low signal to noise it sinks into the noise, while a random wiggle elsewhere looks like a dip. A thin line shows the true, noiseless spectrum for comparison.

The highlighted weak dip exists in the noiseless spectrum (τ > 0.05); this noise draw is fixed by the recorded seed rule.

11Chapter 11: What can we read back?

We fit a few clouds

A fit explains the noisy spectrum with a few smooth clouds, each with an amount, a width and a speed. This noise draw:

Figure: The noisy O VI spectrum at R 30 000 and signal to noise 10, with a smooth fitted curve built from three clouds, each marked by a tick at its speed.

Voigt fit at R 30 000, S/N 10; three precomputed noise draws cycle (first: seed 986299851). Live fits run only in Try it.

The total O VI survives

Add the fitted clouds: total log N = 14.72, against 14.77 from all 672 cells. How much is robust.

Figure: Two bars side by side: the total O VI amount from the fit, 14.72, and the true total from all cells, 14.77, nearly equal.

Turning O VI into total gas needs the oxygen share and the ion fraction, both uncertain.

The clouds are not the cells

One fitted cloud gathers cells tens of kpc apart. A fit tells how much and how fast, not where, and not how hot.

Figure: The threads from the speed map return: one fitted cloud connects to cells scattered tens of kiloparsecs apart along the beam. Then a very broad fitted cloud, and a very narrow invented one.

A fitted width is not a thermometer: at R 15000, S/N 5 one cloud has b = 141 km/s. At R 15000, S/N 20 the fit invents b = 3.1 km/s, far narrower than the blur, so noise made it (total 14.95); no O VI cell on this beam is that cool.
